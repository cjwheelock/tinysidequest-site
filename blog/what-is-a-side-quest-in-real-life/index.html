What Is a Side Quest in Real Life? A Practical Guide

By SideQuest·September 8, 2026

A real-life side quest is a small, optional adventure outside your usual routine. It has a simple goal—notice something, try something, or go somewhere—and a finish you can recognize. It can fit into a lunch break, an errand, or a free afternoon.

You don't need a trip, special equipment, or an impressive story waiting at the end. Walking home by a different public route and finding one detail you've never noticed can be enough.

What makes something a side quest?

The expression comes from games. Cambridge Dictionary describes a side quest as a separate objective outside the main game, and also recognizes its everyday use for an additional activity.

For a real-life version, try three ingredients:

  • A choice: you want to do it, and you can skip it.
  • A little discovery: something about the experience is unfamiliar or worth paying attention to.
  • A clear finish: you know when you've done the thing.

“Be more adventurous” is difficult to start. “Find a piece of public art on the way home” gives you somewhere to begin and permission to stop.

These are invitations, not a required checklist. Change the distance, setting, or activity to suit your time, energy, and access needs.

How to choose your first real-life side quest

  1. Choose your limit. Decide how much time and money you want to spend before choosing the activity. Ten minutes and no purchases is a complete brief.
  2. Pick a verb. Find, notice, taste, ask, draw, and explore suggest something you can actually do.
  3. Add one constraint. Find something purple. Draw one rooftop. Browse one unfamiliar shelf. A small constraint gives the activity a shape.
  4. Write the finish. “After finding three examples” gives the activity a clearer finish than “after exploring enough.”
  5. Keep a small reminder. A sentence, sketch, or photo of an object can be enough. Sharing it is optional.

Your next quest

Find the detail you'd put on a postcard.

Choose somewhere already on your route. Look for one detail that makes it feel like that particular place: a sign, a pattern, a tree, or a window display. Give it a title in your notes. The quest ends when you've picked your detail.

If going out isn't practical, use the same prompt from a window or with an object at home. The interesting part is making a choice about what you notice.

Does it need XP or a reward?

No. You can add points if you enjoy them, but the activity works without a score. A useful reward might simply be keeping the photo, telling a friend what happened, or deciding you'd like to return.

If you add your own XP system, keep it small and optional. Missing a day does not need a penalty. There is no catch-up assignment.

Can you do side quests with friends?

Yes. Agree on the time and budget, choose one shared prompt, and make sure everyone can decline or suggest a different version. A quest about noticing unusual signs gives everyone something to contribute without putting strangers on the spot.

Keep activities in places you're allowed to be. Ask before including another person in a photo or an activity. Discovery does not require trespassing or making someone else part of the game without their agreement.
