What Not to Do in Seattle

By SideQuest·October 24, 2026

Seattle has plenty to offer after the coffee order. Bring a friend, leave some room in the plan, and give the city a little more attention than the inside of your cup.

Start with three small habits: get the Market's name right, share the space around its stalls, and choose one outdoor discovery that isn't another line to stand in.

1. Don't add an apostrophe to Pike Place

It's Pike Place Market. That extra apostrophe can take the afternoon off.

The name appears throughout the Market's own visitor FAQ: Pike Place Market, without the possessive. This is an easy detail to get right, not a vocabulary test you need to pass before enjoying yourself. If you've been saying “Pike's Place,” make the small correction and carry on.

Give the place itself more attention than the naming mistake. The FAQ describes a market with an arcade, lower levels, alleys, and waterfront views. Choose one part that interests you and make time to look around instead of treating the entrance sign as the whole visit.

Before meeting your friend, choose a specific spot and check the current map and access information. “Somewhere at the Market” leaves rather a lot of room for two people to become separate search parties. Agree on a comfortable route and a place to pause.

2. Don't turn a busy aisle into your group's living room

The debate about lunch can have a seated edition.

When you need to regroup, step out of the flow of shoppers and keep counters, entrances, and routes clear. Leave room for people to pass at their own pace. The Market's visitor FAQ links public seating information, so look for a suitable place to sit rather than building a conference around a stall.

The same FAQ welcomes personal photos and video but says tripods aren't allowed for personal use. That's the posted guidance; asking a worker before making them the subject of your photo is another considerate habit. Let them decline easily, and respect any signs or directions at the business.

If someone welcomes a question about what they make or sell, keep it relevant and give paying customers room. A brief conversation can be part of the outing without turning a person at work into your afternoon's entertainment. Check the menu or display before reaching the front, and save the full group deliberation for your pause.

3. Don't let one coffee stop become your whole Seattle story

Enjoy the coffee. Then give your curiosity something else to do.

For a different outing, Seattle Art Museum's Olympic Sculpture Park offers outdoor sculptures on the waterfront with free park admission. Its visitor guidance asks people to stay on paths and walkways and avoid touching or climbing on sculptures.

Choose a piece to look at together, then compare what drew your eye. A curve, a gap, or the way a shape meets the sky can start a conversation without anybody needing to deliver an art-history lecture. Look for the artist's information before guessing what a work means.

Check the park's current hours, map, and access guidance before setting out, and plan for the weather. Treat this as an option for a separate small outing or an addition that fits your energy; you don't need to squeeze the Market and the park into one rushed checklist.

Your optional side quest: trade a tiny sketch

From a public spot where you can pause comfortably, each choose one visible shape and sketch it in thirty seconds. Swap sketches and see whether your friend can spot the detail. Award a point for a surprising choice, not drawing skill.

Use paper or your own phone, keep people and private spaces out of the game, and leave paths clear. A seated version works, too.

SideQuest encourages getting outside, being creative and spontaneous, and interacting with people in the real world. Start with one shared discovery and see where the conversation goes.
