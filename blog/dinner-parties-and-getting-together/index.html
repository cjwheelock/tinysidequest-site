Dinner Parties Are Disappearing. Let's Make Getting Together Easier.

By SideQuest·October 4, 2026

The dinner-party decline is a reason to make getting together easier. A shared walk, a small creative challenge, or a picnic with one friend gives an ordinary day somewhere to go—and someone to share it with.

You can have a group chat full of people you like and still struggle to remember the last time you did something together. Plans stay hypothetical. An evening opens up, and everyone spends it separately.

That's the space SideQuest wants to help change: more time outside in the real world, more curiosity and spontaneity, and more opportunities to interact with people.

What the new dinner-party reporting tells us

In his October 1 article, “Why Americans Stopped Throwing Dinner Parties,” The Atlantic's Derek Thompson examines shrinking home gatherings. Combining historical and current surveys, he reports monthly hosting fell from 42 percent in 1975 to 12 percent in 2026. That is a cross-survey comparison, not a single continuous experiment.

The underlying Data for Progress report compares 1995 findings with a 2026 survey of 1,098 U.S. adults. Its figures measure participation at least once in the previous year:

  • Entertaining people at home: 88 percent to 67 percent, down 21 percentage points.
  • Going on a picnic: 58 percent to 39 percent, down 19 points.
  • Attending a club meeting: 45 percent to 26 percent, down 19 points.

The researchers caution that sampling, survey mode, and weighting differ. Read these as evidence of a broader trend, rather than precise estimates of change.

A smaller plan can still bring people together

A dinner party is a lovely idea. It can also mean coordinating schedules, cooking, cleaning, and finding enough chairs. When the whole thing feels too big, make the invitation smaller.

Pick a public place that's easy for both of you to reach. Set a start time and a comfortable finish. Give the outing one small purpose: find a detail worth sketching, compare two interesting signs, or bring something to eat and catch up.

Try this invitation: “Want to meet at the park entrance Saturday at 11? We can take a twenty-minute walk and each find one thing we'd normally miss.” Adjust the location, time, and route to fit the people you're inviting. A seated version works too.

Give curiosity a job

A tiny mission gives you something to begin with. You can talk about what you notice, trade ideas, and let the conversation wander from there.

  • Find your postcard. Each choose a public detail that captures the neighborhood. Explain your choice when you pause.
  • Make something small. Bring paper and draw the same tree or building detail. Compare what each person noticed.
  • Trade a favorite. Each show the other a nearby place you already enjoy. Keep the distance short enough that there's time to linger.

These are standalone prompts you can try with a friend. SideQuest's real-life quests offer another starting point when you want a little structure for an adventure.

Leave room for a new connection

You can also choose a setting where conversation belongs: a public workshop, a volunteer activity, or a neighborhood event. Check the organizer's current details and go because the activity interests you. If a conversation happens, let it be a bonus.

A friendly question can be enough. Ask another participant what brought them there, or ask a host how someone new can join in. Give people room to engage or return to what they're doing.

Finish with a next time

Before you leave, choose one small thing you'd like to do again. Put it on the calendar while the plan still feels simple. You don't have to organize everyone's social life; you can make one shared hour happen.
