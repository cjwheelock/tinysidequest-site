What Not to Do in Washington, D.C.

By SideQuest·October 20, 2026

Washington, D.C., has room for a small, unhurried adventure. Start by leaving some room for everyone else.

Bring a friend, choose one area, and give yourselves permission to linger. These three habits will help you enjoy the city without turning an ordinary doorway into your group's headquarters.

1. Don't hold a summit at the escalator landing

The debate about which exit to use can wait until you're clear of the escalator.

Metro's escalator safety guidance asks riders to face forward, hold the handrail, avoid running, and leave the landing promptly. Once off, find a clear place away from entrances and the flow of people before opening your map or gathering the group.

The Metro manners guide asks standing escalator riders to keep right. It also says to let passengers off the train before boarding, move away from the doors once inside, and avoid blocking or forcing train doors open.

Give people time and space to move comfortably; nobody needs to race for your itinerary. Anyone can use an elevator, and Metro asks riders to give priority to people who need one for mobility reasons or items that can't go on escalators.

Check your route, current service information, and access needs before setting out. Agree on a meeting spot that doesn't require a conference in the faregate lane.

2. Don't make the Mall your entire map

Enjoy the monuments you came to see. Save some curiosity for the city around them.

For a different starting point, Destination DC's Dupont Circle guide describes bookstore browsing, boutiques, museums, and the neighborhood's namesake fountain. Pick one interest you share and build a small outing around it.

Maybe that's a short stretch of window-shopping followed by a place to sit, or a bookshop browse with each person finding a title to show the other. Check current opening details and agree on any spending before making an indoor stop the centerpiece.

Choose a comfortable walking or rolling distance, use marked crossings, and allow time to pause. You don't need to add another neighborhood just because it appeared on a list. Give this one your attention.

If you meet someone who welcomes a conversation, keep it friendly and brief, and follow their cues. A person working, reading, or heading home doesn't owe visitors an interview. Your friend is already excellent company for discussing the building you both just noticed.

3. Don't turn a neighborhood into your private photo set

A photogenic garage door may still have a job to do.

Destination DC's alley guide describes murals on garage doors and building walls in Shaw's Blagden Alley. It also points out private homes and architects' studios in neighboring Naylor Court. These are places with everyday uses as well as things to look at.

If you choose this area for your outing, enjoy the artwork from public space, follow signs, and keep entrances and vehicle access clear. Stay off private steps and out of private courtyards. Ask before photographing a person, and move on when your pause is blocking someone.

Read the guide's historical context, too. Look for information about a work's artist or the neighborhood rather than treating every interesting surface as a backdrop for the same pose.

Choose Dupont Circle or a Shaw outing according to your interests and energy; these are options, not a requirement to squeeze both into an afternoon. One comfortable stretch and a conversation are plenty.

Your optional side quest: curate a tiny imaginary exhibit

From a public spot where you can comfortably pause, each choose one visible detail: a shape in a building, a tree, a sign, or a pattern. Imagine it belongs in a very small museum.

Give your imaginary exhibit a title and a playful one-sentence label. Award a point for a detail your friend hadn't noticed. Keep the labels clearly invented, leave strangers and private spaces out of the game, and let everyone move at their own pace. A seated version works, too.

SideQuest encourages getting outside, being creative and spontaneous, and interacting with people in the real world. Start with one shared outing and see what catches your attention together.
