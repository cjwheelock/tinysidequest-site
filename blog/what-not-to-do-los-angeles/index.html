What Not to Do in Los Angeles

By SideQuest·October 14, 2026

Los Angeles is a good place to make your plan smaller. Choose one area, leave room for a detour, and remember that a great view still has neighbors—or cyclists—around it.

These three habits are worth skipping on an outing with a friend. Bring curiosity and enough time to actually enjoy where you are. Your afternoon doesn't need a sequel in every neighborhood.

1. Don't mistake a zoomed-out map for a short trip

Hollywood, Downtown, and Venice can look wonderfully convenient when your map is small enough. Before promising all three in an afternoon, check the actual journeys for the time you plan to travel.

Los Angeles Tourism's planning guidance recommends grouping activities by neighborhood and accounting for traffic. Pick one main area and one thing you're excited about. Give yourselves time to arrive, find your way, pause, and change your minds.

For a smaller starting point, browse Discover LA's Little Tokyo guide. It describes this Downtown neighborhood as a Japanese American cultural and civic center, with cultural attractions, restaurants, and shops. Choose a short section that interests both of you; you don't need to complete the whole guide.

Agree on a comfortable walking or rolling distance, access needs, and any spending. Check current hours before counting on an indoor stop. Leave a little unscheduled time to read a public sign or compare something you've noticed. If you're enjoying the conversation, staying put is a perfectly respectable itinerary update.

2. Don't cast somebody's home as your Hollywood backdrop

A famous view doesn't make a driveway a visitor lounge.

For the Hollywood Sign, start with the Hollywood Sign Trust's visitor information and its authorized hiking routes. The Sign itself is in a restricted area, and visitors cannot approach or touch it. The Trust also warns that some nearby residential parking is permit-only and asks drivers to check signs.

Use a listed viewpoint or an authorized trail that fits your group. Keep residential entrances and driveways clear, stay out of private property, and save the elaborate photo direction for somewhere you aren't in anyone's way. Ask before photographing a person. Being excited about the view and considerate of the neighborhood can happen in the same frame.

If you choose a hike, plan it as a hike: check conditions, bring water, and allow enough daylight. The Trust's trail guidance says to stay on authorized routes. A distant view is also a successful visit; nobody has to earn their photo through a climb they didn't want.

3. Don't turn Venice's bike path into a photo stop

The ocean makes an excellent background. It makes a poor excuse for forgetting the path around you.

LA County's Venice Beach guide identifies Ocean Front Walk as the promenade alongside the beach and lists the bike trail separately. Use the pedestrian promenade for your stroll, follow local signs and marked crossings, and keep the cycling path clear when you stop to look around.

Find a safe place out of the flow before checking your phone or taking a photo. Leave room at entrances and curb ramps, keep your group from spreading across a narrow passage, and give everyone time to move at their own pace. If you're riding, stay attentive and follow the posted route rather than weaving through people.

A good beach outing can be one comfortable stretch and a place to sit together. Look around, talk, and take your trash with you. The sunset doesn't improve because you rushed to collect three more stops.

Your optional side quest: invent the opening credits

Choose a public spot where you and a friend can comfortably pause. Each find two visible details—a color, a building shape, a tree, or a sign—and imagine they belong in the opening credits of a movie.

Give your imaginary film a title and a one-sentence premise. Award a point for each different detail you noticed, then pitch your ideas to each other. No filming strangers, entering private spaces, or standing in traffic required. A seated version works, too.

SideQuest encourages getting outside, being creative and spontaneous, and interacting with people in the real world. One neighborhood and one shared idea give you plenty to start with.
