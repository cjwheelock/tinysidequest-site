What Not to Do in New Orleans

By SideQuest·October 22, 2026

New Orleans deserves your curiosity. It also deserves a visitor who can tell the difference between a great night out and somebody else's front doorstep.

Bring a friend and leave room for an unplanned discovery. These three habits can help you enjoy the music, notice the neighborhood, and participate with a little more care.

1. Don't give every block the Bourbon Street treatment

Your outside voice doesn't need an all-access pass.

The Music and Culture Coalition of New Orleans, or MaCCNO, reminds visitors in its Good Visitor's Guide that tourist destinations are also neighborhoods. Keep the late-night conversation low as you head back, and leave doorways and residential steps to the people using them.

Give yourself another way to meet the city, too. The official Frenchmen Street guide describes music clubs as well as a daytime look at the street's architecture. Frenchmen also extends beyond its entertainment stretch into residential Gentilly; a street name isn't a promise that every block is a nightlife district.

Choose one manageable stretch for a daytime wander with your friend. Compare a building detail you each noticed, then decide together whether to keep going or find somewhere to sit. Stay on public routes, keep entrances clear, and plan around your own walking or rolling needs. A short outing still counts as an outing.

2. Don't make the band compete with your group chat

The story can wait until the song finishes. It may even improve with editing.

MaCCNO's visitor guide asks listeners to move toward the back for conversation, ask before taking a performer's photo, and avoid flash. It also encourages tipping musicians. Budget for supporting the music you enjoy rather than assuming a performance is simply part of the scenery.

Before choosing a show, check the venue's current admission, age, access, and photography policies. Settle those details with your friend so nobody arrives expecting a different evening. Follow the rules of the room once you're inside.

Try giving one song your full attention. Afterwards, each describe one thing you heard: an instrument answering another, a change in rhythm, a moment that surprised you. You don't need musical vocabulary to have something interesting to share.

If you want to learn more, look for the artist's own page or a published interview. Save your questions for an appropriate, welcomed moment; someone doing their job doesn't owe you a personal lesson.

3. Don't assume every procession is yours to join

A brass band is a reason to pay attention, not automatic permission to step into the middle.

The official second-line history traces the tradition to Black neighborhood celebrations and social-aid organizations. Wedding processions and jazz funerals carry their own occasions and meanings.

MaCCNO's parade guidance distinguishes participatory parades from ritual processions such as jazz funerals. For the former, keep toward the back, clear of the band and parade members, and move when asked. Observe ritual processions from the perimeter without interrupting for a photograph.

For a planned outing, WWOZ's Takin' It to the Streets publishes second-line schedules and route information. Check the current listing and organizer guidance; don't infer the nature of an event from its soundtrack.

If you're unsure, observe respectfully and leave space. You can enjoy a shared discovery without making yourself its main character.

Your optional side quest: make a sound postcard

At a public spot where you can comfortably pause, each notice three sounds. Afterwards, give your little collection an imaginary album title and explain your choice. Award a point for a sound your friend missed.

Skip recording strangers, interrupting performers, or turning a procession into a game. A seated version works, and nobody needs to hurry.

SideQuest encourages getting outside, being creative and spontaneous, and interacting with people in the real world. Pick one small outing, invite a friend, and give it your attention.
