What Not to Do in Boston

By SideQuest·October 16, 2026

Boston invites you to look closely. Just try to do your looking somewhere other than the middle of a doorway.

A good visit doesn't require a perfect accent, an encyclopedic knowledge of the Revolution, or a friend willing to march through every stop on your list. Start with a small outing and these three habits to skip.

1. Don't navigate every neighborhood as if it's the same grid

Your confident announcement that the next stop is ‘just three blocks that way’ deserves a quick map check.

Boston has grids, but they aren't one consistent citywide pattern. Boston.com's reporting on street orientation explains the mix of street directions and differently aligned grids. Back Bay is a useful counterexample to the idea that every Boston street is crooked: the Leventhal Map & Education Center's historical exhibition describes its planned grid around Commonwealth Avenue.

Choose one area and a starting point you can both find. Check the actual route rather than assuming a turn will keep you moving toward your destination. Pause out of the pedestrian flow to consult your phone, and use marked crossings instead of improvising a diagonal shortcut.

Leave room to change the plan. If a public sign, an interesting building, or a place to sit catches your attention, you can stop there. A wrong turn doesn't need to become a forced march to rescue the itinerary.

2. Don't board the first Green Line train on color alone

Green is a helpful clue. It is not your complete itinerary.

The MBTA's subway guide explains that the Green Line has four branches. Before boarding, check your route, the train's destination, and station signs. Use the guide's trip-planning resources to confirm where you need to go, and check current service information before setting out. Plan for your group's access needs, too.

Once the train arrives, let people off first, then move inside rather than gathering at the doors. The MBTA also asks riders to keep bags out of the way, use only one seat, offer seats to people who need them, and use headphones in stations and vehicles.

Keep your friend's recap of the afternoon at conversation volume. Nobody else in the car needs the director's commentary. If you're unsure where to go, stop somewhere clear of the flow and ask MBTA staff for help.

3. Don't treat the Freedom Trail as your group's private lane

The red line is a guide, not a reservation.

The National Park Service's Freedom Trail guide describes a 2.5-mile route through Boston and Charlestown, largely along city sidewalks. It also notes stairs and sections requiring alternate routes. Look at the accessibility information before choosing your stretch.

Make room for people going about their day. Gather out of the way before reading a plaque, taking a photo, or debating the next stop. Keep entrances and curb ramps clear, leave passing room, and let everyone move at their own pace.

You can pick a handful of sites instead of completing the whole trail. NPS explicitly offers that approach and notes that individual sites may charge admission. Check current opening and access details before counting on an indoor visit. One place explored with attention beats a checklist you barely remember.

Your optional side quest: spot the detail your friend missed

Choose a short public stretch or a comfortable place to sit. Each quietly find three details: a shape, a texture, and something written. Take turns giving one clue while your friend tries to spot the same thing. Award a point for each discovery.

Use visible buildings, signs, trees, or public artwork. Keep strangers and private spaces out of the game, and pause somewhere clear of traffic. No speed bonus; noticing is the point.

SideQuest encourages getting outside, being creative and spontaneous, and interacting with people in the real world. Invite a friend, choose a manageable outing, and leave some room for whatever you notice together.
