The Case for a Small Detour

By SideQuest·October 4, 2026

A small detour can give an ordinary day a new detail: a doorway you had never noticed, a corner with a different view, or a place you would like to show someone. You can find that change close to home.

Familiar routes have their uses. They get you to work, the grocery store, or the bus without much planning. But when there is room in the day, it can be interesting to ask what is just outside the usual route.

You do not need to turn every errand into an expedition. Choose one manageable variation, and give curiosity a little space.

What researchers found about daily variety

A 2020 study in Nature Neuroscience tracked young adults' locations and asked about their feelings over three to four months. The analysis included 122 participants in New York and Miami. On days when people visited a more varied set of locations, they tended to report more positive feelings.

The study found an association, rather than testing a randomly assigned detour. Its results also suggested the relationship could run both ways: feeling better may encourage exploration, and varied experiences may accompany feeling better. It does not establish that taking a new route will improve everyone's mood.

The practical idea is an invitation to try something different and decide whether you enjoy it. There is no score for the number of places you visit, and no need to make a busy day busier.

Change one part of a familiar outing

Keep the destination and change an easy part of the journey. Take another known public route, enter a park through a different open entrance, or pause in a spot you normally pass.

You could also keep the route and add one nearby stop: a public artwork, a library display, or a community noticeboard. Check opening hours, access, and any admission costs before making a special trip. Choose a variation that fits the time and energy you actually have.

A useful limit might be ten extra minutes or one additional stop. That limit is a suggested way to make the outing manageable, not a finding from the research. A detour can stay small enough that you still get home when you intended.

Play the one-new-detail game

Before you begin, choose one thing to look for: unusual lettering, a repeating color, an interesting roofline, or a public sign that answers a question. Find one example you had not noticed before.

Pause somewhere suitable and give the discovery a title. Write a sentence, make a quick sketch, or simply remember what caught your attention. The quest ends with that one detail.

The aim is to notice the place around you. Stay in public areas you are allowed to use, leave people their privacy, and keep doorways and busy paths clear. If a route does not suit you, change the game or choose a comfortable seated observation spot.

Let a friend choose the next turn

Agree on the route, return time, and spending limit together. Then each choose one nearby detail to show the other. It can be a favorite spot for one person and a discovery for the other.

Try choosing a theme before you leave, such as interesting doors or things that resemble animals. Compare what each of you notices. If a conversation takes over, let it. The prompt has done its job.

You can suggest another outing around something you found: a library program to look up, an artwork to revisit, or a question about the neighborhood's history. Check the organizer's current information before turning a notice into a plan.

Make room for curiosity in an ordinary week

Afterward, ask whether there is one thing you would return to or share with someone. Some detours will be interesting. Others will just be a different way home.

One new detail is enough for today. Tomorrow's curiosity can choose its own direction.

Source

Aaron S. Heller and colleagues, Association between real-world experiential diversity and positive affect relates to hippocampal-striatal functional connectivity, Nature Neuroscience, 2020.
